# Best in Causal Span

## A non-comparative account of novelty, and what it does to the problem of evil

Jarrod Hamilton (alisru)
08 September 2026

---

## Abstract

I set out and defend a thesis I call BCS, best in causal span. The thesis is that anything new is the exact output of exactly what was present at the moment of its making, and that "best" applied to such a thing is not a comparative grade but an identity claim. Nothing falls short, because there is no co-available alternative it fell short of.

The thesis has one central structural consequence. It is a claim about the boundary of what can be produced at a moment, not a claim about which point inside that boundary was reached. This single distinction does two things at once. It dissolves an entire family of complaints about the world, including the standard problem of evil, by showing them to be boundary complaints disguised as interior complaints. And it leaves moral evaluation completely intact, because moral evaluation lives entirely inside the boundary, where genuine alternatives exist and are declined.

A second consequence follows once the account is extended to cover information as well as material. The information state at any moment is itself a made thing, produced by the moment before it, so no agent can act on a state better than the one its history has produced. Every act is therefore performed on a partial state, which makes suffering, understood as acting with a lack of understanding, a property of sequencing rather than an occasional intrusion into it. That result supplies a production mechanism the bare definition lacks, and it applies symmetrically to all parties, including those aiming at what they take to be good.

BCS is a backward-looking operator throughout. Run forward it would be fatalism; run backward on a fully specified frame it is a completeness claim, which is why it cannot function as an excuse.

I state BCS formally, prove five results from four axioms, distinguish it from four neighbouring positions it is likely to be confused with (Leibnizian optimism, Kauffman's adjacent possible, Simon's satisficing, and Panglossian adaptationism), test it against five case studies in metallurgy, physics, biology, technology and medicine, and then apply it to the problem of evil, to Rowe's fawn, to van Inwagen's no-minimum result, and to the omnipotence objection. I close by stating the price of the thesis honestly, which is a God who does not intervene selectively, and by showing what evaluative work remains once BCS has done its work.

## 1. Introduction: the question this answers

There is a question that keeps arriving in different clothes.

Why is the world not better than it is? Why did the good thing take so long? Why was it not just made right the first time? Why does an all-powerful maker allow suffering that seems to serve no purpose? Why does progress crawl through error when the answer was, in principle, always available?

These look like several questions. They are one. Each of them asserts, without arguing for it, that a better outcome was available at the moment in question and was not taken. That assertion is the whole load-bearing member. Remove it and every one of these questions changes from an accusation into a description.

My claim is that the assertion is almost always false, and false for a structural reason rather than a contingent one. What was available at any moment was bounded by what existed at that moment. Not bounded loosely, bounded exactly. And within that bound, what came out was the exact output of what went in.

I call this BCS, best in causal span. It applies to a hand axe, a theory of gravitation, a species, an institution, a person, and to the universe itself, which was new once and is not exempt from the rule that governs newness.

The word "best" in the name is doing unfamiliar work, and I want to flag the unfamiliarity immediately rather than let it cause trouble later. "Best" here is not a superlative reached by comparison. It is not the winner of a contest. It carries no praise. It marks the absence of a shortfall, which is a different thing from the presence of excellence. A stone that fell to the bottom of a well did not perform well. It also did not fall short. BCS says that made things are in the position of that stone with respect to the grading scale most people reach for, and in a quite different position with respect to a scale I will identify in section 6.

## 2. Informal statement

Anything new can only be made out of what already exists.

This is not an observation about scarcity or difficulty. It is a claim about what making is. To make is to take material that is and put it into a state it was not in. There is no other operation available to a thing that is itself inside time. Something that could bring a state about without material would not be making, it would be positing, and I will argue in section 9 that only one kind of thing could do that, and that it is the one thing that is not inside the sequence at all.

From that, the rest follows quickly.

At any moment, the set of things that can be produced is fixed by what is there. Call that set the span. The span is not a rough estimate of what was feasible. It is exact. Everything in the state of affairs at that moment counts, and nothing outside it counts at all.

What actually comes out of that moment is the exact resultant of the causes operating on exactly that material. Perfect cause, perfect effect, no remainder, no waste, no missing part. Not "roughly what was available", exactly what was available.

Therefore "best" here is not a mark on a scale that runs from bad through adequate to ideal. There is no ideal sitting outside the span to be measured against, because an ideal made of material that does not yet exist is not an alternative, it is a description of a different world with a different history. Grading against it is not a harsh judgement, it is a category error.

Therefore iteration is not the slow route to a result. It is the only route by which the material of the result comes into being. An end state cannot precede the things it is made of, and those things are themselves end states of earlier moments, each of which was the exact output of its own span.

And therefore the question "why was it not just made right" is not asking why the fast way was declined. It is asking why an end can exist before its parts.

## 3. Formal statement

I will state the thesis with enough precision to be attacked properly, because a thesis that cannot be attacked cannot be defended either.

### 3.1 Axioms

**A1, Material Closure.** Every made thing is made from what already exists. Nothing enters a making from outside the state of affairs obtaining at the time of the making.

**A2, Causal Sufficiency.** Every event is fully accounted for by what preceded it. There is no residue in an effect that is not traceable to its causes.

**A3, Finitude.** Every made thing occupies, consumes, or excludes some portion of what exists. Nothing finite can come to be without something else being unable to be, in that place, of that material, at that time.

**A4, Epistemic Closure.** The information held at a moment is itself a made thing, and is therefore subject to A1. Knowledge is produced from what was already present, by operations that were themselves produced. No agent can step outside its information state at the moment of acting in order to act on a better one, because acquiring information is an act, made from what is there.

A4 closes a gap that A1 alone leaves open. Without it, an objector can grant that the material was constrained and then say the maker simply needed to know better, treating knowledge as a free variable that could have been set higher at no causal cost. It could not. The information state at t is the output of the span at t minus one, exactly as the material is.

A1 and A3 are widely accepted and I will not labour them. A2 is contested, and I will treat it carefully, because the strength of my results turns on which reading of it I need. I will show in section 3.4 that the central result survives the weak reading, which is the reading a libertarian about free will can accept.

### 3.2 Definitions

**D1, State.** Σ(t) is the total state of affairs at time t. This includes material, arrangement, capacities, and whatever knowledge is instantiated in the things present.

**D2, Span.** S(t) is the set of things producible from Σ(t) by any operation that satisfies A1. The span is the reach of a moment.

**D3, Making.** A making is a transition from Σ(t) to some x present at a later time, where x was not present at t.

**D4, BCS.** For any made thing x arising from Σ(t): x is a member of S(t), and x is the resultant of the causes operating on Σ(t).

### 3.3 Theorem 1: Non-comparativity

*Claim.* Where A2 holds in its strong form, there is no y such that x fell short of y.

*Proof.* "x fell short of y" requires three things to be simultaneously true. First, that y was producible from Σ(t), that is, y is in S(t). Second, that the causes operating on Σ(t) could have produced y. Third, that they produced x instead. Under strong A2, the causes operating on Σ(t) produced exactly x, without remainder. Any y distinct from x therefore requires causes distinct from those operating on Σ(t). But those causes are constituents of Σ(t). Distinct causes therefore entail a distinct state, Σ'(t), and hence a distinct span, S'(t). So y is a member of S'(t), not of S(t). The second condition fails. There is no shortfall. ∎

*Corollary.* Where strong A2 holds, "x is the best in its causal span" and "x is what came out of its causal span" are the same statement. The evaluative-sounding claim carries no evaluative content. This is a feature, not an embarrassment, and section 6 explains why.

### 3.4 Theorem 1b: The boundary result

The interesting question is what survives if A2 is read weakly, so that Σ(t) underdetermines what follows and more than one member of S(t) was genuinely available.

*Claim.* Even under weak A2, no y outside S(t) is an alternative that x fell short of.

*Proof.* Under weak A2, the second condition of the shortfall claim may be satisfiable: the causes at t may have been able to produce some y other than x. But this is only possible for y within S(t), since by A1 nothing outside S(t) is producible from Σ(t) at all. For any y outside S(t), the first condition fails directly. ∎

This is the load-bearing result of the whole paper, and I want to name it plainly.

**BCS is a boundary thesis, not an interior thesis.** It is silent about which point in the span was reached. It is absolute about where the span ends.

Everything BCS dissolves, it dissolves by showing the complaint to be a boundary complaint. Everything BCS leaves standing, it leaves standing because the complaint is an interior complaint. The two look identical when stated in ordinary language, which is why the confusion is so durable.

### 3.5 Theorem 2: The necessity of iteration

*Claim.* If x requires material m, and m does not exist at t, then x is not producible at t, and no quantity of power, knowledge or intention changes this.

*Proof.* By A1, x is made from what exists at t. If m is not in Σ(t), then m is not available to the making. If x cannot be made without m, x is not in S(t). Since m is itself a made thing, m has its own material requirement m', and so on. Therefore the production of x requires an ordered sequence of makings, each of which brings into being the material of the next. ∎

*Corollary.* Iteration is not a method chosen over a faster alternative. It is the mechanism by which the material of any end state comes to exist. There is no version of the end that arrives before the sequence, because the end is made of the sequence's outputs.

### 3.6 Theorem 3: Dissolution of the perfection complaint

*Claim.* The complaint "state P should have obtained at t0" is either an interior complaint, in which case it is legitimate and BCS does not touch it, or a boundary complaint, in which case it is a request to suspend A1.

*Proof.* Either P is in S(t0) or it is not. If it is, the complaint is that an available alternative was declined, which is exactly the shape of an ordinary moral or practical criticism, and Theorem 1b leaves it fully intact. If P is not in S(t0), then by A1 P was not producible from Σ(t0), and the complaint asserts that it should have been produced anyway. To produce something not made from what exists is to violate A1. ∎

*What suspending A1 costs.* If effects need not follow from their material, then no state is made of any prior state. If no state is made of any prior state, there is no sequence in which one thing leads to another. Growth, choice, consequence and time as succession all require that later states be constituted out of earlier ones. Remove A1 and what remains is not a kinder world running on the same rules. It is a still image. Movement and exposure are the same thing described from two sides.

So the choice presented by the boundary complaint is not between this world and this world improved. It is between a world that runs on causality and a world that runs on fiat. Those are different worlds, not versions of one.

### 3.7 Theorem 4: Suffering as a property of sequencing

I define suffering as acting with a lack of understanding. It sits in the act, not in a sensation, which means it is never located in whoever it lands on. Pain lands. Suffering is done. I have argued for that definition elsewhere and here I take it as given, because what follows is a consequence of it rather than a defence of it.

*Claim.* Under A4, suffering is not an occasional event within the sequence. It is a property of sequencing itself.

*Proof.* By A4, the information state at any t is the output of the span at t minus one, and is therefore finite and partial: it contains what has been produced and nothing else. No agent at t possesses the information that later spans will produce, since that information does not yet exist to be possessed. Every act issuing from t is therefore an act performed on an incomplete information state. By the definition above, an act performed with a lack of understanding is suffering. Therefore every act at every frame is done with some lack of understanding, and suffering is produced continuously rather than intermittently. ∎

Three things follow, and they matter more than the proof.

**It supplies a mechanism the account otherwise lacks.** The definition says what suffering is. This says why it is produced at every frame rather than at some frames, and the answer is not malice, scarcity or misfortune. It is that acting from a partial state is the only kind of acting available inside time.

**It relocates the question.** If suffering were an occasional intrusion, the right question would be why it intrudes, which is the question the problem of evil asks. If it is a property of sequencing, that question has no purchase, and the live question becomes how much of it a given act does relative to what the frame permitted, which is an interior question of exactly the kind section 6 handles.

**It is symmetrical across actors.** Two parties can each be aiming at what they take to be good, each acting on the best state available to them, and harm is produced anyway, by both, with no override anywhere in the chain. This is the ordinary case rather than the tragic exception. Where it looks like one party is aiming at good and the other at bad, that reading is usually itself an artefact of a partial information state, held by whoever is doing the reading.

### 3.8 The direction of application

BCS is a backward-looking operator, and applying it forward inverts its character. The distinction is worth stating explicitly because the same sentence does opposite work in the two directions.

**Applied retrospectively**, to a frame that is now completely specified, BCS says that what issued from that frame was the exact output of it, that no other outcome was available from that exact material, operations and information state, and that the event was therefore necessary in the only sense of necessity that causality supports. This is a completeness claim about the frame. It is not a verdict on the outcome and it is not an excuse for anyone.

**Applied prospectively**, to a frame not yet specified, the same words become fatalism, and fatalism is false here for a structural reason rather than a moral one. From inside a moment, the frame is not fully specified, because part of what specifies it is what the agent is about to do. An agent reasoning "whatever I do will have been the exact output" is not reading the frame, it is declining to be one of the things that constitutes it.

This is why BCS cannot function as an excuse, and the reason is formal rather than rhetorical. An excuse is prospective. It is offered by an agent about a choice they are making or are about to make. A completeness claim about a fully specified frame is not available to an agent standing inside an unspecified one. By the time the claim is true, the acting is over and there is nothing left to excuse.

It is also why the interior evaluation of section 6 survives intact. Interior evaluation is done retrospectively, on the same completely specified frame, and asks a different question of it: given everything that was in that frame, where in the span did the output land. Both questions are asked backward. They are not in competition.

## 4. What BCS is not

BCS sits near several established positions. The distinctions matter, because BCS is easily mistaken for each of them, and each mistaking makes it either trivial or false.

### 4.1 It is not Leibnizian optimism

Leibniz argued that God, having before him infinitely many possible worlds, and being bound by the principle of sufficient reason to have a reason for the choice, must have chosen on the ground of perfection, and therefore this world is the best of all possible worlds (Leibniz 1710; *Monadology* §§53 to 55). Britannica's summary of the argument makes the structure clear: the conclusion is reached from the divine attributes rather than from a survey of the world's contents.

This is a comparative claim across a global set. It requires that the comparison class exist, that its members be commensurable, and that a maximum exist within it. Each of those requirements has been attacked. Aquinas had already held that God can always make something better than what he has made, though only by making more things, since "the present creation being supposed, cannot be better" (*Summa Theologiae* I, q.25, a.6). Plantinga's version of the objection is that for any world one can conceive a better, for instance one containing one more righteous person, so there is no best. Rowe turned the point into an argument against theism, and Leftow's reply concedes the shape of the problem while rejecting the key premise (Leftow 2005).

BCS makes no claim of this kind and is not exposed to any of these attacks. It does not say this world beat the others. It says the comparison class, as the objection needs it, is not there. Leibniz compares complete world-histories selected all at once. BCS operates on single moments and asks only what was producible from the material then present. Leibniz's "best" is a superlative. Mine is the absence of a shortfall.

The two theses also have opposite relations to optimism. Leibniz's conclusion is that the world is as good as a world can be. Mine implies nothing whatever about how good the world is. It only says that a particular way of finding it wanting does not work.

### 4.2 It is not the adjacent possible

Kauffman introduced the adjacent possible to describe how biological evolution explores what is available to it at each stage, defined as the set of possibilities open to an entity at a given point in its development (Kauffman 1996, 2000; Björneborn 2020). Kauffman's biospheres, on his account, enter their adjacent possible as fast as they can sustain, and each entry reconfigures the space, so the topology of what comes next is not statable in advance.

The boundary in Kauffman's account and the span in mine are the same boundary. I take that as confirmation rather than as a problem. Where the accounts part is in what they are for.

Kauffman is describing a space of options and the dynamics of exploring it. His question is what can happen next. My question is about the standing of what did happen: whether it is gradeable, and against what. Kauffman's adjacent possible is a set to be searched; BCS adds the claim that the output of the search is not a failed approximation of anything outside the set. That claim is evaluative rather than descriptive, and Kauffman does not make it. It is also the claim that does the work against the problem of evil, which Kauffman is not addressing.

So the honest positioning is this. BCS takes a boundary that complexity theory already describes, adds a thesis about the logic of "best" across that boundary, and turns the result on a theological and moral problem that the original was never pointed at.

### 4.3 It is not satisficing

Simon's satisficing agent searches available alternatives until one crosses an aspiration level and then stops, because the ideal optimisation the classical model demands is not a procedure any finite agent can actually run (Simon 1955, 1956, 1979). Bounded rationality is not a claim that people are irrational; it is a claim that no organism has the memory or time that maximisation presumes.

Satisficing is about a threshold accepted in place of an optimum. It presupposes that the optimum is real and out of reach. BCS denies the presupposition. There is no optimum sitting outside the span, imperfectly approached. Simon's agent settles; the span does not settle for anything, because there was nothing else to settle away from.

The two do meet in one place. Simon's constraint is the agent's capacity to search; mine is the world's stock of material. An agent can improve at searching. Nobody can improve at having material that does not exist.

### 4.4 It is not Panglossian adaptationism

Gould and Lewontin attacked an adaptationist programme that broke organisms into traits and told a separate adaptive story for each, with trade-offs as the only brake on perfection, so that non-optimality itself came out as a result of adaptation (Gould and Lewontin 1979). They argued instead that organisms must be read as wholes whose body plans are so constrained by inherited history, developmental pathways and general layout that the constraints matter more than the selective forces.

This is easy to confuse with BCS, because both are constraint-first positions and both refuse to grade against an unreachable ideal. The difference is that Gould and Lewontin were attacking a claim of optimisation, whereas BCS is not making one. Their target was the belief that what exists is the best solution to a problem. BCS says nothing about solutions or problems; it says what exists is what the material admitted. If anything, Gould and Lewontin's constraint programme is evidence for the span, since developmental constraint is what a span looks like when a biologist describes it.

### 4.5 It is not fatalism, and it is not quietism

This is the objection that will occur to most readers first, so it gets its own section.

## 5. The trivialisation objection

The objection runs: if "best" just means "what happened", then BCS is a tautology wearing a theodicy's clothes. It approves of everything by defining disapproval out of existence. It cannot criticise the Holocaust, a bad policy, or a broken promise, because each of these was the exact output of its own span.

If BCS said that, it would deserve the charge. It does not say that.

BCS does not claim that what happened is good. It claims that what happened is not gradeable against alternatives that would have required different material. That is a restriction on the class of admissible counterfactuals, not a verdict on outcomes.

Theorem 1b is where this bites. Under the weak reading of A2, spans contain more than one member. When a span contained a better option and a worse one was taken, the criticism is exact, it is legitimate, and BCS is entirely silent about it. The Holocaust is not defended by BCS in any respect. Every alternative to it, at every moment of its planning and execution, sat squarely inside the span of that moment. The material existed. The knowledge existed. The capacity to do otherwise existed. It was declined. That is an interior failure and it is as damnable as it has always been.

What BCS forbids is a different sentence, one that looks the same in English: "the eleventh century should have had antibiotics." Nobody declined antibiotics in the eleventh century. There was nothing to decline.

So the effect of BCS on moral judgement is not to soften it. It is to aim it. Complaints that were previously scattered across the whole surface of history get sorted into two piles: those where an available alternative was refused, which is where blame belongs and where it now sits undiluted, and those where the alternative was never there, which stop being accusations and become descriptions.

I would put it more strongly. Diffusing blame across the boundary is what protects the interior failures. If everything from the fall of Rome to yesterday's bad decision is one undifferentiated field of disappointment, no particular refusal stands out. BCS clears the field so the refusals are visible.

## 6. Where the evaluation actually happens

If BCS empties out one scale, it needs to say what scale is left. Otherwise it has removed a bad instrument and supplied nothing.

Two measures survive, and both are strictly interior.

**The first is position within the span.** Given what was actually there, what came out? This is a real question with a real answer whenever a span holds more than one option, which is most of the time for anything with agency. It is where every ordinary moral judgement lives.

**The second is the effect on future spans.** Every making adds its output to what-is, and the next span is drawn from that. So an act can be assessed by what it does to the reach of what comes after. This is not a consequentialist tally of outcomes; it is a structural question about whether the span is widened or narrowed.

That second measure gives the correct treatment of taking, which is the point at which people usually try to smuggle the fantasy world back in.

By A3, nothing finite exists without taking. Eating a plant ends a living process. The plant, before that, was drawing on soil, water and light that something else could have used. Occupying ground is itself a form of taking, since ground used for one thing cannot be used for another. Even photosynthesis draws down a supply the plant did not make. There is no way to be a finite thing in a finite place that does not exclude something.

The instinct that herbivory escapes this is the boundary complaint again, one round further in. It does not escape it, and the attempt to find something that does escape it terminates in section 9.

But it does not follow that all taking is equivalent, and this is where the second measure does its work. Taking is assessable by two properties of what is taken.

**Renewability**, whether what is taken regrows within the time that matters.

**Rarity**, how scarce it is relative to what the surroundings need to remain viable.

Taking from something abundant and fast-regrowing and taking from something scarce and slow-regrowing are different acts, though both are literally taking. The first leaves the next span roughly intact. The second narrows it. That is a real difference, it is measurable in principle, and it is not a difference in the act's necessity, since both are equally unavoidable in kind.

Mapped onto the two-axis measure I use elsewhere, where υ tracks who an act benefits and ψ tracks what the energy is doing, taking does not sit automatically at the destructive end of ψ merely because it is unavoidable. Where it sits depends on whether the take outruns what can regrow, given how scarce the thing actually is there. Viability decides the placement, not the bare fact of taking.

So the evaluative remainder after BCS is not thin. It is the whole of practical ethics, minus one move that never worked.

## 7. Case studies

BCS makes a testable-looking claim about the history of made things: that at each stage, the material of the next stage was not present, and was produced by that stage. Five cases, chosen so that four support the boundary claim and the fifth shows the interior claim doing independent work.

### 7.1 Bronze and iron

Bronze was not a failed attempt at iron. The temperatures involved are not close. Wrought iron work needs furnace conditions far above those bronze requires, on the order of 1480 degrees Celsius against roughly 920 (University of Ottawa, Museum of Classical Antiquities). The bloomery that eventually produced usable iron depends on prepared charcoal, which is close to pure carbon and supplies both the heat and the carbon monoxide that reduces the ore, on tuyeres admitting air, and on bellows or draught to force it (Wikipedia, Bloomery, drawing on standard metallurgical sources). Early European bloomeries were small precisely because human-powered bellows and hand-driven hammers set the limit.

The practice that built furnaces, worked out draught, and made charcoal reliably at scale was bronze-working practice. Iron was not something the Bronze Age fell short of. Iron was not there to fall short of. Bronze was the exact output of its span, and part of that output was the material of iron's span.

The later history repeats the pattern one level up. Successive improvements in heating from around 1000 BCE onward produced the higher temperatures that blast furnaces need, and it was those that made carbon-alloyed steel and large-scale casting available. Blast furnaces dedicated to molten iron were not built until the fourteenth century.

### 7.2 Newton and Einstein

Newtonian mechanics was the exact output of what could be observed in 1687. General relativity required three things that did not exist then, and could not have.

The anomaly in Mercury's perihelion was first identified as a problem in celestial mechanics by Le Verrier in 1859, from a re-analysis of timed transit observations running from 1697 to 1848, giving a discrepancy from Newtonian prediction of about 38 arcseconds per century, later revised upward by Newcomb (Wikipedia, Tests of general relativity; Rydin 2009). That re-analysis required a century and a half of accumulated positional astronomy. It could not have been done sooner because the observations did not exist.

The Michelson-Morley result came in 1887, performed between April and July at what is now Case Western Reserve and published that November (Michelson-Morley experiment; Britannica). It required an interferometer sensitive enough to detect a null. Michelson's own 1881 attempt was, in his view, insufficiently sensitive.

Maxwell's equations were in place by then. General relativity was published in 1915 into a setting that held the anomaly, the null result, and the field equations of electromagnetism together.

None of that existed earlier. Newton was not wrong and was not slow. The earlier state was not a worse version of the later one. It was what the later one is made of.

### 7.3 Biological lock-in

Carnivores did not choose meat in any deliberate sense. A lineage's body and gut specialised around available fuel, and once specialised, the route back closed.

Cats are the clean case. They cannot synthesise taurine in sufficient quantity and must obtain it from the diet, and the experimental work shows that putative precursors do not rescue them: cats fed cysteinesulfinic acid showed no significant increase in taurine in plasma, whole blood or muscle (Edgar et al. 1998). They also cannot make arginine, and cannot convert beta-carotene to vitamin A as dogs and humans can, so they require retinol from meat. Alongside the metabolic losses sit sensory ones: the sweet receptor gene *Tas1r2* has been examined across Carnivora precisely because of this pattern of loss and indifference to sweet stimuli (Li et al. 2009).

The general principle has a name. Dollo's law of irreversibility, from Louis Dollo in 1893 and formalised by Simpson, holds that an organism cannot return to a state already realised in its ancestry, and that lost complex traits do not reappear in their original form (Elmer and Clobert 2025; Marshall 1994). The modern refinement is not that the law is false but that its mechanism is genetic and developmental: once the developmental pathway or the genetic architecture underlying a complex trait is lost, reappearance is prevented, even where individual genes survive through pleiotropy.

Jacob's framing predates all of this and is still the clearest. Evolution does not work like an engineer, who obtains materials and tools fitted to a task; it works like a tinkerer, using whatever is lying about, so that constraint and history explain the shape of life better than optimisation does (Jacob 1977).

"Choice" in the carnivore case therefore means accumulated causal commitment. That is causality running over generations, and the resulting animal is the exact output of the span its lineage occupied.

### 7.4 Technological lock-in

David's account of QWERTY is the canonical case in economics: a technology that historiography judges inefficient endures because early decisions constrain later ones, an effect he named path dependence and lock-in (David 1985; Hall, Dominguez Lacasa and Günther 2011 for the critique). Whatever one thinks of the specific keyboard claim, and it has been contested, the structural point is well established and is the same point as the biological one. The set of decisions available now is limited by decisions already made, even where the original circumstances no longer obtain, so the present is never a clean slate.

This is the span, described by economists rather than by biologists.

### 7.5 The interior case: Lind and Semmelweis

The four cases above show the boundary. This one shows the interior, and it matters because it is the case BCS does not excuse.

James Lind ran his comparative trial aboard the *Salisbury* between 20 May and 17 June 1747 and reported that oranges and lemons cured scurvy. The Royal Navy did not adopt citrus rations until 1795 (Sutton 2003; Tröhler 2003). The year after Lind's *Treatise* appeared, the Navy's Sick and Hurt Board rejected a proposal to supply sailors with fruit juice. Tröhler is careful to note that the Board's early caution was not simply obtuse: Lind's was one treatise among many, lemon juice was not new, and Lind himself also recommended vegetables that modern analysis says were ineffective. But the delay runs to nearly five decades, and by 1815 Blane could show scurvy had all but vanished from the fleet.

Semmelweis is starker. In 1847 he ordered examiners at the Vienna General Hospital's First Clinic to scrub in chlorinated lime, and average monthly maternal mortality fell from 10.65 per cent for January 1841 to May 1847, to 1.98 per cent for June 1847 to February 1849, a risk ratio of 5.38. The result was rejected.

In both cases the material was present. The lemons existed. The chlorinated lime existed. The comparative evidence existed and had been produced. The alternative sat inside the span and was declined.

This is exactly the shape of judgement BCS preserves and sharpens. Neither case is excused by anything in this paper. The eleventh century's lack of antibiotics and the 1750s Navy's lack of citrus rations are not the same kind of absence, and the whole point of the boundary distinction is that they are told apart.

## 8. Application to the problem of evil

### 8.1 The necessary and unnecessary collapse

The standard complaint is not about suffering as such but about suffering held to be unnecessary. Rowe's formulation is the most cited: an omnipotent being is incompatible with gratuitous evils, defined as evils that could have been prevented without losing some greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil, and there are such evils, therefore no such being (Rowe 1979).

"Necessary" means caused by what came before it. Inside a world that runs that way, "unnecessary suffering" is not a subclass of suffering; it is a description of suffering whose scope, magnitude, method, cause or effect someone objects to. The word "unnecessary" only carries weight if a second world is quietly introduced, one where the suffering could be removed without changing anything else.

Under Theorem 3, that introduction is the boundary complaint. It requires that a state be produced without its material, which is a suspension of A1, which removes succession, which removes the world being complained about.

Theorem 4 makes the same point from the other direction, and the two together close the position. The complaint treats suffering as an intrusion into an otherwise unsuffering sequence, so that the right question is why it was let in. If suffering is instead what acting from a partial state amounts to, and if by A4 no state is ever complete, then there is no unsuffering sequence for it to have intruded upon. The question "why is there any" has the same standing as asking why a sequence has succession in it.

### 8.2 Rowe's fawn

Rowe's example is a fawn dying slowly in a forest fire, unobserved, apparently pointlessly. He writes that the answer to whether an omnipotent being could have prevented it is obvious, and later that it certainly seems to us pointless (Rowe 1979, 1979 and 2006 as quoted in McAllister et al., forthcoming).

BCS does not deny the fawn's pain, does not minimise it, and does not offer a compensating good. It asks a narrower question: was the prevention inside the span?

Prevention would require one of a small number of things. That the fire not start, which requires the antecedent conditions of that fire not to obtain, which is a different state and therefore a different span. That the fawn be elsewhere, likewise. Or that the pain be suspended while everything else proceeds, which is the override, and I will come to that.

Note what BCS does not do here. It does not claim, as the skeptical theist does, that we are not in a position to see the greater good that the suffering serves (Wykstra 1984; Alston 1991; Bergmann 2001). That reply concedes the shape of Rowe's argument and pleads epistemic limitation. BCS declines the shape. There need be no greater good, because there was no available alternative that the fawn's death was the price of.

### 8.3 Van Inwagen's no-minimum, reached differently

Van Inwagen argues that no non-arbitrary line can be drawn marking the minimum amount of evil a perfectly good and all-powerful God would be permitted to allow, and that this defeats the local argument from horrors (van Inwagen 1988, 1991, 2006). His route is through vagueness: any proposed minimum is arbitrary in the way sorites boundaries are arbitrary, and only an epistemicist about vagueness escapes, at a price he thinks too high.

BCS reaches a related conclusion by a different route and, I think, a cleaner one. The problem with the minimum is not that the line is hard to place. It is that the request presupposes a dial: a quantity of suffering that could be set independently of the causal chain producing it. There is no dial. Suffering is not a level applied to a world; it is what specific chains of causes produce. Asking for less of it, holding everything else fixed, is asking for a chain whose material differs while its output does not.

Van Inwagen's result is that we cannot say where the line goes. Mine is that there is no dimension along which to place one.

### 8.4 The omnipotence objection

The objection is that an omnipotent being allowing suffering is a contradiction, since omnipotence covers the alternative.

The objection hides an assumption: that divine power names a stock of unexercised options sitting outside what was actually made, a "could have" beside the "did". If what exists is not separate from the power, then the causal structure of the world, predation included, is not a failure to use the power. It is the power in use. The objection only works if the alternative world is already real enough to be withheld.

There is a standard limit here that most parties already accept. Almost every philosopher and theologian has held that omnipotence does not extend to the logically impossible, and the point matters: if God could do the logically impossible, Rowe's argument would be inert anyway, since the impossibility of allowing gratuitous evil would be no hindrance to allowing it (McAllister et al., forthcoming).

My claim is that "produce an end state before its material exists" belongs to that family. It is not a hard task. It is not a description of a task.

**The conjuring concession.** An objector can press: surely a sufficiently powerful being could conjure a working transistor into 1850, atom for atom, without semiconductor industry or quantum theory. I concede this fully. Nothing in A1 forbids positing; A1 governs making.

The concession is harmless, and seeing why is instructive. What the theodicy objection actually asks for is not a scattering of conjured objects. It is a world that developed better. A world of conjurings is not that. In it, nothing is produced by anything, so nothing follows from anything, so there is no sequence of one thing leading to another. It is the still image again, arrived at by a different door.

So the concession loops back into the same disjunction. A world that makes, or a world that posits. Not one with the other's advantages.

**Constructor theory as a modern parallel.** Deutsch and Marletto propose that physical laws be expressed entirely in terms of which transformations are possible and which impossible, and why, replacing the usual pairing of initial conditions with laws of motion. A task is impossible if a law of physics forbids performing it with arbitrary accuracy, and possible otherwise, with no third option (Deutsch 2012; Deutsch and Marletto 2015).

I am not claiming BCS is constructor theory, and I do not want the borrowed authority. I am pointing at a structural similarity worth noticing: a dichotomy between possible and impossible transformations, with no intermediate category of "possible but not achieved because the maker fell short". That is the same refusal of the shortfall grade that Theorem 1 delivers.

The convergence goes one step further, and this one surprised me. Deutsch draws from his dichotomy the conclusion that all evils are caused by lack of knowledge, and that the converse holds: all evils are soluble given the right knowledge. I arrived independently, from a different starting point, at the position that suffering is acting with a lack of understanding, which places it in the act rather than in the sensation. I note the convergence without leaning on it. Two routes reaching the same place is weak evidence, but it is not no evidence.

### 8.5 The selective miracle objection, and the price

This is the strongest objection remaining, and I will not dodge it.

Why not causality with occasional overrides? A world that runs on causes, but where the worst outcomes are quietly prevented. This does not freeze anything. It looks like it gets everything.

The reply is that it changes what problem we are solving, and hands it straight back to Rowe.

If overrides are permitted, they are part of the state of affairs, and the span includes them. The question then recurs one level up: which overrides, and why these and not others? An objector who allows overrides needs a non-arbitrary override policy. Van Inwagen's result says none is specifiable. So BCS plus overrides collapses into exactly the position the standard debate is stuck in, and none of the work done above survives the collapse.

So the price is explicit, and I would rather state it than have it found.

**BCS buys the dissolution of the problem of evil at the cost of a God who does not intervene selectively.** Anyone who requires selective intervention should reject BCS. It is not compatible with a maker who sometimes reaches in, and it does not become compatible by making the reaching in rare.

That is a real theological cost. It is close to the cost paid by classical accounts of secondary causation, where God is the primary cause of the being of everything and creatures are secondary causes whose activity reaches particular effects and depends on divine action, so that God does not substitute for creatures except in miracles (Aquinas, as summarised in Artigas 2003). BCS takes that account and removes the exception clause. Aquinas's own image is apt even so: nature is a plan of art placed within things themselves, as if a shipwright could give the timbers the power to move by themselves into the form of the ship (Aquinas, *Commentary on Aristotle's Physics*).

## 9. The uncaused term

BCS has a boundary condition of its own, and it generates a familiar result without being designed to.

By A1 and A3, every made thing takes from what already is. Either the sequence of takings runs back without end, or it terminates in something that makes without taking. Something that makes without taking depends on nothing prior to itself. Call it the uncaused term.

Three things about this result matter.

**It is a requirement, not an identification.** BCS shows the slot exists. It does not fill it. The uncaused term could be God, understood as ground of being. It could be a brute initial condition with no further explanation. It could be an unresolved feature of a beginningless chain. BCS is neutral, and its neutrality is a strength: the boundary results in sections 3 and 8 hold on any of these readings, which means the argument is not smuggling theism in as a premise.

**It sits well with what the classical tradition already said.** Aristotle argued that actuality is prior to potentiality even in time, since the seed is preceded by an adult of the same kind, and that what is eternal must be fully actual, since what exists only potentially is capable of not being and hence perishable (*Metaphysics* Θ.8, 1049b to 1051a). Aquinas took this over as *actus purus*, pure act, actuality unmixed with potentiality. That is what an uncaused term would have to be: nothing in it waiting to be brought about by something else.

**It explains why taking is not a defect.** Taking is not a flaw that slipped into the design. It is what being a finite thing that persists through time consists in. Only the uncaused term makes without taking. Everything else has to take, because everything else is made of what came before. Taking is the price of anything besides the uncaused term existing as a separate thing in time at all.

Whitehead's account is the nearest process-metaphysical neighbour, and the comparison is worth drawing. His actual occasions are events rather than enduring things, each arising by concrescence out of its prehension of prior occasions, so that becoming is a creative advance into novelty (Whitehead 1929). That is the span, at the finest grain. Where I part from Whitehead is that his God supplies each occasion with an initial aim, a lure toward the best possibility available to it, which reintroduces selection at every step. BCS does not need the lure and does not want it, for the same reason it does not want overrides: the moment something is being steered toward a best available option, the question of why that option and not another is live again.

## 10. Objections and replies

**"This is unfalsifiable."** The boundary claim is not. It says that at the moment of any given making, the material of the alternative did not exist. That is a historical claim, and section 7 is a set of tests it could have failed. The metallurgy case would fail if bronze-age furnace practice turned out to be irrelevant to iron. The relativity case would fail if the Mercury anomaly and the interferometry result had been available and ignored. Section 7.5 shows what a failure looks like, since Lind and Semmelweis are cases where the material was present.

**"It licenses inaction."** It does the opposite. The only thing it removes is complaint aimed across the boundary, which is inert anyway since nothing follows from it about what to do. What it leaves is the interior question: given what is actually here now, what is in the span? That question is exhausting rather than comforting, because it removes every excuse of the form "the conditions were not right" in cases where the conditions were.

**"The span is not sharply bounded, so the whole thing is vague."** The span's edge is fuzzy in practice, in the way any historical counterfactual is fuzzy. But the argument does not need a sharp edge. It needs only cases that are clearly outside, and there are plenty: antibiotics in 1080, transistors in 1850, general relativity in 1687. The dissolution in section 8 works on the clear cases, and the unclear cases are where interior evaluation resumes, which is the right place for them.

**"You have proved too little. Nobody thinks God should have given the eleventh century antibiotics."** They think something with the same shape. The complaint that a world should have arisen containing agents who never harm, or matter that never scarcifies, or bodies that cannot fail, is the boundary complaint at world scale. The eleventh-century antibiotics case is the small, obvious instance of a move that is much harder to see when it is made about the world as a whole.

**"This makes the world's badness unaddressable."** No. It makes one bad addressing method unavailable and points at two live ones: position within the span, and effect on future spans. Section 6 is where that work goes, and it is not less demanding than what it replaces.

**"Determinism does the same job more simply."** It does not, and it is worth being clear why. A fully determined world with no chance anywhere is not automatically a safe one. A lion killing a gazelle can be entirely fixed by what came before and still be exactly as violent. Determinism makes suffering certain rather than merely likely; it does not remove it. So chance is not the thing that needs removing. What actually freezes a world with no harm in it is the requirement that predation, scarcity and death all need more than one thing to exist and need one of them to want what another has. Take that away and nothing can happen to anything, determined or not. Determinism only enters afterwards, as the patch required if you kept many interacting beings and still wanted zero harm guaranteed: every event would have to be locked to a script, since one loose moment breaks the rule. That is determinism patching a world already frozen, not the thing doing the freezing.

## 11. Conclusion

BCS says one thing, and the rest is consequence.

What is new can only be made from what already is, and what comes out is the exact output of exactly that. So "best in causal span" is not a grade awarded after a comparison. It is the observation that the comparison has no second term.

The single most useful thing the thesis does is separate two complaints that ordinary language runs together. One says an available alternative was declined. The other says an unavailable alternative should have been produced anyway. The first is the whole of moral and practical criticism, and BCS leaves it untouched and better aimed. The second is a request that effects stop following from causes, which is a request for a different world, and not a version of this one with a fix applied.

The problem of evil, in nearly every form it takes, is the second complaint wearing the clothes of the first. Rowe's fawn, the demand for a minimum, the objection from omnipotence, the appeal to a kinder universe: each of them needs an alternative that was co-available with the world's actual material, and none of them supplies one.

What is left over is not small. Taking is unavoidable, but taking is not one thing. What regrows in time and what does not are different, and the difference is measurable. Where a span held more than one option and the worse was taken, that is a failure, and it is now more visible rather than less, because it is no longer buried under a general disappointment with history.

The last thing worth saying is what BCS does not deliver. It does not show the world is good. It does not compensate anyone for anything. It does not supply a reason why any particular suffering had to occur, and it explicitly declines the skeptical theist's move of gesturing at goods we cannot see. It shows that one specific accusation cannot be made, and it leaves the world exactly as it found it, minus one argument.
