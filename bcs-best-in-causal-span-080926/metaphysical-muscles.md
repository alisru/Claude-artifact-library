# Metaphysical Muscles

## Bones, Marrow, and the Weight of a Concept

Jarrod Hamilton (alisru)
September 2026

Companion to *Best in Causal Span*. That paper establishes that every change on every body of a thing requires force, that force comes from a tool, and that tools are made, finite, and in most cases built by use. This document develops what that means when the tool is a person's own capacity, and gives the anatomy.

---

## 1. The Claim

A concept has weight. The weight is not intrinsic to the concept. It is relative to the strength of the specific muscle it lands on.

Repentance is light for some people and unliftable for others, and the difference is not in repentance. Surrender, forgiveness, admitting error, changing a foundational belief, tolerating an unresolved question, taking a loss without retaliating: each of these is a weight, and each lands on a particular band of a particular muscle, and whether it can be lifted is a fact about that band in that person at that moment.

This is the direct consequence of *Best in Causal Span* section 4. Force comes from a tool. The tool is finite. It was built by use from wherever it already was. So a person's capacity to move on any body of themselves is a strength that stands at some level, and a demand made of them is a load that either falls within that level or does not.

The word "can't" is used in this document literally. It does not mean "won't" or "finds it hard." It means the load exceeds the muscle, in the same sense that a bar can exceed a lifter.

---

## 2. The Anatomy

A body has bones, marrow and muscle, and the metaphysical body has the same three, doing the same three jobs.

### 2.1 Bones: The Foundation of Belief

Bones are what bears load. They are the foundation of belief: the commitments a person stands on, that everything else attaches to, and that are not themselves moved by ordinary use.

A bone is not a conclusion. It is structural. A person does not reason their way to their bones and cannot easily reason their way off them, because reasoning is one of the things the bones hold up. This is why arguments against someone's foundation so rarely move it. The argument is a load applied to a muscle, and the foundation is not a muscle.

Bones can be broken. A broken foundation is a different injury from a weak muscle, and it does not heal by training. A person whose foundation has been fractured, by loss, by betrayal, by discovering that something they stood on was not there, cannot lift their way out of it, because lifting is done by muscles and muscles attach to bones. Training a muscle on a broken bone produces further damage.

### 2.2 Marrow: The Information Structure Inside the Bone

Marrow is inside the bone. It is the information structure that generates sustenance and heals damage originating at the foundation.

Two jobs. It produces what the rest of the body runs on. And it repairs the bone it lives in.

In the metaphysical body, marrow is the interior account a person holds of why their foundation is what it is: not the foundation itself but the understanding that sustains it and can rebuild it after damage. A foundation held without marrow is brittle. It bears load until it cracks, and then there is nothing inside to knit it. A foundation held with marrow can crack and heal, because the person has an account of what the bone was for and can reconstitute it.

This is why two people with the same beliefs respond differently to the same blow. The bones are the same. The marrow is not. One has an information structure that can regenerate the foundation from the inside, and one has a foundation that is only a foundation.

Marrow is information, in the sense of *Best in Causal Span* section 2.3. It is made, it is bounded by what the person has been able to acquire, and it cannot be supplied from outside by telling someone what their foundation is for. It has to be grown, and it grows from what is already there.

### 2.3 Muscles: Aspects Relative to Use

Muscles move the bones. They are the capacities built by use, and they are the part of the anatomy that is *for* something: for moving the legs, for lifting, for acting.

A muscle is relative to use in two senses. It is built by the use it is put to, so a person has the muscles their history required of them and not others. And it is specific to a kind of movement, so strength in one band does not transfer to another. A person can be enormously strong on one body of themselves and have never developed the band that a particular concept lands on.

The seven bodies of *Best in Causal Span* section 3 are seven muscle groups. Identity, potential, location, meaning, logic, history, consequence. Each is trained separately, each has bands within it, and each has a strength that was built or left unbuilt.

## 3. The Weight of a Concept

### 3.1 Weight Is Relative

The same concept is a different weight for different people, and the difference is entirely in the muscle.

"I was wrong" is a load on a specific band. For a person who has lifted it a thousand times in small increments, it is nearly weightless. For a person whose whole standing has depended on never having lifted it, it is not heavy. It is beyond the muscle, which is a different condition, and the person cannot lift it any more than they can lift a car.

This is not a claim about willingness. Willingness is on the Who body and is itself a muscle. It is a claim about whether the load falls within the range of the specific band, and a load outside the range cannot be lifted by wanting to.

### 3.2 The Bar That Cannot Be Lifted

The clearest cases are the ones where a person's whole trajectory would have been different had they lifted one weight, and they did not, and the reason was capacity.

Hitler never repented and never surrendered. On the account here, the reason is not that he considered those options and rejected them. It is that he completely lacked the capacity, physical or metaphysical, to lift that bar. Repentance and surrender each land on a band, and in him those bands had never been built, and by the point at which lifting would have mattered, everything else in him was structured around their absence. The weight was not merely heavy. It was outside the range of the muscle, and no amount of will on the Who body changes the range of a band on the Effect body.

This is not exculpation and *Best in Causal Span* section 8.1 gives the reason it cannot be. It is a description of the mechanics. What it removes is the assumption that the alternative was available to him and declined, which is the assumption under which the failure looks like a choice.

A judge who says he should have surrendered is demanding a lift from a band they have not measured, and section 1.4 of the companion paper describes what that judgment reports about the judge.

### 3.3 Increments and Injury

Muscles are built in increments the muscle can currently sustain. Loading beyond that does not accelerate development. It produces injury.

So the person who cannot lift "I was wrong" does not get there by being told to lift it. They get there, if at all, by lifting something in the same band that is within their range, and then something slightly heavier, over a long time, with the muscle developing between lifts. This is *Best in Causal Span* 4.2, self-developing tools, and it has the same shape: you cannot train from a strength you do not have.

It also explains a common injury. A person pressed to lift a weight beyond their band, by circumstance or by someone who has mistaken the weight as light because it is light for them, does not lift it and does not merely fail to. Something tears. What tears is usually attachment: the muscle's connection to the bone, which is to say the capacity's connection to the foundation. After that the person is not merely weak on that band. They are unable to load it at all until the attachment heals, and healing is marrow's work, not training's.

### 3.4 Atrophy and Loss

Muscles that are not used shrink. Bands that were once strong and have not been loaded in years are not where they were, and the person often does not know this until they reach for the capacity and find it gone.

This is *Best in Causal Span* 4.5, tools lost, at the scale of a single person. The record of having once been able to lift something is not the ability. A person who could once admit error easily and has spent a decade in a position where admitting error was never required has a different band than they remember having, and the first real load will show it.

## 4. Order That Reads as Chaos

The person who appears to act for no reason is the one whose muscles are hardest to read from outside, and the account here says why the reading fails.

An actor who sets out to produce disorder has muscles, bones and marrow like anyone else. What they are doing is highly ordered: a consistent programme, executed with capacities built for it, resting on a foundation that supports it, sustained by an interior account of why. What the observer cannot see is the foundation, because it is not the observer's, and without seeing the foundation the movements look arbitrary.

This is the structure that the pairing of a figure who imposes order and a figure who presents as chaos keeps being used to examine. The one who presents as chaos is not without a programme. Their programme is the demonstration that the other's order is contingent, and that demonstration is carried out with precision and consistency, which is to say with strong muscles on specific bands. The chaos is the reading, not the actor.

The general lesson is the one *Best in Causal Span* 1.4 draws. Reported absence of reason measures the distance between the observer's state and the actor's, not the absence of a chain. When an act reads as chaos, the observer is looking for bones they can recognise, finding none, and concluding there are none. The bones are there. They are a different shape.

## 5. Diagnosis

When a person cannot do something, the anatomy gives three distinct places the failure can be, and they need different responses.

**The muscle is weak.** The band the load falls on has not been built to that range. Response: load it within its current range and build. Time is the constraint and nothing shortens it.

**The muscle is torn.** The band was loaded beyond range and the attachment failed. Response: stop loading it. Healing is marrow's work and happens from the inside, and training before it is healed makes it worse.

**The bone is broken.** The foundation the muscle attaches to has fractured. Response: no amount of muscle work helps, because there is nothing for the muscle to pull against. Marrow has to knit the bone, and if the marrow is thin, the person's interior account of what the foundation was for has to be grown before anything else can happen.

The most common error is treating all three as the first. A person who cannot lift a weight is told to try harder, which is a prescription for building a weak muscle and an injury for the other two conditions. The diagnostic question is not "why won't they" but "where is the failure," and the answer is not readable from the outside, for the reason section 4 gives.

---

## 6. What This Adds to the Companion Paper

*Best in Causal Span* establishes that force comes from a tool and the tool is finite. This document gives the tool an anatomy, and the anatomy does three things the bare claim does not.

It distinguishes three failures that look identical from outside and need different responses.

It explains why arguments do not move foundations. Arguments are loads on muscles, and foundations are bones.

And it makes the weight of a concept a measurable relation between a load and a specific band, rather than a property of the concept, which is what turns "they should have" from an accusation into a claim about capacity that can be checked, and usually cannot be sustained.
