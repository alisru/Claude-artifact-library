# Best in Causal Span

## An Account of Causality, Limits, and Human Action

Jarrod Hamilton (alisru)
September 2026

---

## Abstract

This paper presents Best in Causal Span (BCS), an account of causality, material limits, and human action across time. BCS holds that any new entity, event, or decision can only be assembled from the exact physical materials, operational tools, and information present in its immediate prior state.

The three inputs are not a list. They are the three relations anything made stands in to what made it: what it is made from, what it is made through, and what was present to inform it. Each is itself a made thing, produced by an earlier state and limited by it.

The paper argues that this constraint holds not only for matter but on every kind of state a thing can occupy: identity, potential, location, meaning, logic, history and consequence. On each of these nothing jumps. Every change is a continuous translation through intermediate states, driven by force supplied by a tool that is itself finite and had to be built. Where the force is absent the state remains fixed, however open the path.

From this follow six results. The origin of the universe is the one state with nothing before it, and the ordering of what emerges from it is fixed rather than chosen. Historical actors could not have acted on knowledge they did not hold, which is a claim about ability rather than a plea in mitigation. Suffering is a structural feature of sequential time rather than a defect in it. Forgiveness has a mechanical basis and exactly one boundary, and that boundary is definitional rather than moral. The capacity to forgive is distinct from the forgivability of the act, and is a trained capacity rather than a conclusion. And mistakes are real while regret, in its strict form, is incoherent.

## 1. The Rule

### 1.1 Statement

Anything new can only be assembled from what is already present in the immediate prior state.

Every event and every decision is the exact causal output of that state. Not approximately and not mostly. Exactly, because the state is what the output is made of.

### 1.2 Why This Is Not Trivial

Stated baldly, the rule invites the response that it is obviously true and therefore says nothing. That response mistakes the scope.

Read as a claim about matter alone, the rule is close to conservation and nearly nobody disputes it. Read at its actual scope, it covers four things that are routinely treated as though they were free variables: the operations available to an actor, the categories under which an actor can recognise anything, the information an actor holds, and the strength of the capacities an actor brings to bear. Each of these is a made thing with a production history, and each is bounded by that history exactly as matter is bounded by conservation.

The substantive claim is that these are not exempt. Most counterfactual reasoning about the past proceeds by holding matter fixed and varying one of the four silently. "They had everything they needed" almost always means the matter was present, and almost never means the operation, the category, the information and the capacity were all present too.

### 1.3 What "Best" Means Here

In BCS, "best" does not mean morally approved, well-executed, admirable, or good. It names an exact causal resultant.

An outcome is the best in its causal span because, given the precise configuration of matter, tools, conditioning and information present at that instant, no other co-available output could have occurred. The superlative is doing the work of an identity claim, not a ranking.

The terminology is awkward and the awkwardness is worth stating plainly, because the obvious reading of "best" is evaluative and that reading is wrong in every instance in this paper. The reason for keeping the word is that the alternative formulations lose something. "The only output" suggests a bare determinism and drops the exactness of fit between inputs and result. "The optimal output" reintroduces the ranking. What "best in causal span" names is the output that used precisely what was there, with no remainder and nothing wasted, which is a property of the fit rather than of the outcome's value.

A worked instance: a bridge collapses under load. The collapse is the best in its causal span. That means the steel, the loads, the corrosion and the design constraints produced exactly that failure and could not have produced another. It does not mean the collapse was good, acceptable, or the best available engineering outcome. Those are different questions about different objects.

### 1.4 Causality Is Information Moving Forward

A past state is complete. Nothing can be added to it, because everything it was going to produce has been produced.

The present is where assembly happens, and it is the only position where a state is incomplete and something is being done anyway.

Causality is the flow between them. What a completed state holds becomes the material of the state being assembled now, and that one becomes material for the next. Nothing enters the flow from outside it.

This is why BCS evaluates completed states only. The claim it makes is about the fit between an output and its inputs, and until the output exists there is nothing for the claim to be about. Section 8.1 draws the consequence for excuses.

### 1.5 What BCS Is For

BCS is not a system of moral judgment and does not sort blame into categories. It is a claim about the mechanics of causality, and its use is narrow.

Its use is to eliminate a class of counterfactual claim, of which the clearest instance is "the eleventh century should have had nuclear power."

Every claim of this type needs an alternative to compare against. Specifying that alternative means introducing materials, tools or knowledge that were not present, which changes the state, which means the comparison is no longer with this reality but with an invented one that has an unrelated causal lineage. The claim has the grammar of a criticism and no second term.

Alternative history serves fiction. Treated as analysis it halts discourse, because the invented branch can be specified to yield whatever the arguer wants and there is no fact that adjudicates between specifications.

The class is larger than it looks. "The eleventh century should have had nuclear power" is transparently empty. "The 1940s should have had modern moral sensibilities," "that institution should have known better," and "I should have seen it coming" are the same move with the missing input hidden rather than obvious.

### 1.6 Relation to Existing Accounts

BCS occupies ground adjacent to several established positions and is not identical to any of them. Naming the differences is faster than letting them be inferred.

**Leibnizian optimism.** Leibniz argued that God surveyed infinitely many possible worlds and actualised the best, so that this world is the best of all possible worlds. That is a comparative claim across a global set, requiring the set to exist, its members to be commensurable, and a maximum to exist within it. Aquinas had already denied the last, holding that God can always make something better than what he has made. BCS makes no claim of this kind. It does not say this world beat the others. It says the comparison class the objection requires is not available, because any alternative requires different inputs and therefore a different state. Leibniz's "best" is a superlative reached by comparison. The "best" here is the absence of a shortfall, which is compatible with the outcome being terrible.

**Kauffman's adjacent possible.** Kauffman described biological and technological evolution as exploring the set of possibilities open to a system at a given point, with each entry reconfiguring the space so that the next set is not statable in advance. The boundary Kauffman describes and the boundary here are the same boundary, and that convergence is confirmation rather than a problem. The accounts differ in what they are for. Kauffman describes a space of options and the dynamics of searching it, which is a descriptive project about what can happen next. BCS asks after the standing of what did happen: whether it is gradeable, and against what. That is an evaluative question Kauffman does not raise.

**Simon's satisficing.** Simon argued that agents accept an outcome crossing an aspiration level rather than optimising, because optimisation is not a procedure a finite agent can run. Satisficing presupposes that an optimum is real and out of reach. BCS denies the presupposition: there is no optimum outside the state being imperfectly approached. The two meet in one place, which is that both are constraint-first accounts of action, and they differ in what is constrained. Simon's limit is the agent's capacity to search. The limit here is what the state contains.

**Gould and Lewontin on constraint.** Their attack on the adaptationist programme argued that inherited history, developmental pathways and general body plan constrain organisms more than selection shapes them. This is evidence for the account rather than a rival to it: developmental constraint is what a bounded state looks like when a biologist describes it. The difference is that they were attacking a claim of optimisation, whereas BCS makes none.

**Frankfurt on alternative possibilities.** Frankfurt-style cases argue that an agent can be morally responsible for an act even where they could not have done otherwise. This matters here because BCS denies that alternatives were available, and a reader will reasonably ask what becomes of responsibility. Section 8 treats it. The short form is that BCS does not need to establish responsibility, because it is not in the business of assigning it, but it also does not destroy the grounds Frankfurt identifies.

## 2. The Three Inputs

The assembling state is not a pile of atoms. Three kinds of thing are in it, and each is itself made and therefore limited.

### 2.1 Materials

Matter and energy already present in the local state. Nothing enters from outside it.

Every physical entity also occupies space, consumes energy and excludes alternatives. Two bodies cannot occupy one coordinate. To build, sustain or occupy anything is to draw on finite resources thereby made unavailable to other configurations. This is the least contentious of the three inputs and the argument for it is not developed here, because nothing in the paper turns on a reader disputing it.

### 2.2 Tools

A tool is a made thing, so the rule applies to it exactly as to anything else.

Anything usable as a tool is of the universe to begin with. There is no lever standing outside a state, and no instrument that was not produced by an earlier one. Every means of changing a state is already inside the state being changed.

The claim that needs argument is that an operation is a tool in the same sense that a hammer is.

Consider what it takes to arrive at the number one as a quantity that can be manipulated. An actor needs zero, which is a concept with a documented history of invention and diffusion rather than a fact available to anyone who reflects. The actor needs a notion of unit, of increment, and of the intermediate values between them. And the actor needs addition, which is an operation with a definition and a procedure and not a self-evident feature of the world.

None of these was waiting in empty space to be noticed. Each was constructed, transmitted and had to be learned. An actor holding all the raw material for a machine but lacking the procedure that combines it is as constrained as one holding no material at all, and the constraint is of the same kind rather than a metaphorical extension of it: in both cases a necessary input to the making is absent from the state.

A reader may object that operations are discovered rather than made, so that the truths they encode were always available even if nobody had them. Grant the point about the truths. It does not help, because what an actor uses is not a truth but a procedure they can execute, and procedures are learned, practised and held by particular people at particular times. Availability in principle is not presence in a state, and BCS is a claim about states.

Section 4 treats tools at length, because they are what supplies force, and force is what every change requires.

### 2.3 Information

An actor's knowledge at any moment is bounded by the inputs, conditioning and history accumulated up to it.

The load-bearing claim is that an actor cannot step outside their information state in order to act on a better one. The argument is that acquiring information is itself an act. It requires attention, time, prior categories under which the new information can register, and usually instruments. All of those are in the state. So the operation that would improve the state is itself performed by the state, and cannot exceed it.

This closes an exit that A1 alone leaves open. It is easy to grant that an actor was constrained in materials while treating their knowledge as a variable that could have been set higher at no causal cost, as though knowing better were an act of will. It is not. The information state at any moment is the output of prior states exactly as iron or stone is.

Information includes individuation. An entity or problem cannot be aimed at, solved or manipulated until it has been picked out and recognised as a distinct thing, and picking out is work done with a tool. This is developed in 3.5, because its consequences run through the other bodies.

### 2.4 From, Through, and Present At

The three inputs are not an arbitrary list, and the reason there are three rather than two or five is worth stating.

They are the three relations anything made stands in to what made it. What it is made **from**, which is the material it is constituted of. What it is made **through**, which is the instrument by which the material is worked. And what was **present at** the making to inform it, which is the state of knowledge under which the working was directed.

That these are three distinct relations rather than aspects of one is shown by their independence. A state can hold all the material and no instrument, which is the eleventh century and antibiotics. It can hold material and instrument and no correct individuation of what it is working on, which is section 5's account of Semmelweis. It can hold a correct account and a strong instrument and no material, which is most of engineering before industrialisation. Each combination is a real and distinct condition, which is what it means for the relations to be independent rather than three names for one requirement.

**The Trinitarian mapping.** The same threefold division appears in Trinitarian theology, mapped as materials to the Father, tools to the Son, and information to the Spirit. The correspondence is not loose, and the first two legs are carried by prepositions rather than inference.

1 Corinthians 8:6 marks the distinction with two different Greek words. Of the Father: ἐξ οὗ τὰ πάντα, from whom all things. Of the Son: δι' οὗ τὰ πάντα, through whom all things. ἐκ is out-of, denoting source, what a thing comes from. διά with the genitive is through, denoting agency and means. John 1:3 uses the same διά of the Son: all things were made through him.

So the Son leg is not the pattern, the plan, or the blueprint. It is the instrument, which is what section 4 means by a tool and what 4.1 means in saying there is no route around one. The reading is not an imposition on the text: it is the preposition the text uses, and the distinction between the two prepositions is precisely the distinction between constitution and agency.

The Spirit leg is placed at the origin state directly. Genesis 1:2 has the earth formless and void, darkness over the deep, and the Spirit of God hovering over the waters. Only then, in 1:3, does God speak. Information is present before anything is made, which is what section 6.1 says of the origin state on independent grounds.

Two clarifications keep the mapping from claiming more than it should. First, it is not a sequence of stages in which one person acts and then another. The three are conditions that must all hold for anything to be made at all, and a state short on any one produces nothing. That simultaneity is what makes the mapping compatible with the three being coequal rather than ordered. Second, classical Trinitarian theology treats such attributions as appropriations, ways of speaking suited to our understanding rather than divisions of labour within the divine nature, and the mapping here should be read at that strength.

Nothing later in the paper depends on the mapping. A reader who rejects it can drop 2.4's second half and lose nothing downstream. It is included because the threefold division of from, through and present-at is not an invention of this paper, and a division arrived at independently in two places is weak evidence that it tracks something real about making.

## 3. The Seven Bodies

### 3.1 The Generalisation

The rule is not a rule about matter. It governs every kind of state a thing can be in.

A thing has seven bodies, and on each of them the same constraint holds. No state on a body is reachable from another without passing through every intermediate state, and passing through requires force applied by that body's own tool. Without the force, the state stays where it is.

Nothing teleports, and not on any body.

The generalisation is the substantive move in this section, and the argument for it is this. The impossibility of teleportation across space is not a fact about space in particular. It is a consequence of the rule: a state at a location is assembled from the state that preceded it, and a state at a distant location shares no constitutive continuity with it, so there is nothing for the later state to be made from. Wherever that structure holds, the same impossibility holds. And it holds on every body, because every body is a kind of state, and every state is assembled from the state before it.

What varies between bodies is not whether the constraint applies but what counts as an intermediate state and what counts as force.

The seven are stated below under the 42-structure, in which they comprise one emergent driver and three paired axes. The pairing is not decorative: each axis holds two bodies that trade off against one another, which is what makes a state's shape readable in the way 4.3 describes.

### 3.2 The Driver

**Who. Identity and will.** An actor cannot jump from one state of identity or will to another. Becoming someone who acts differently is not an act of resolution. It is a translation through intermediate conditioning, driven by effort, necessity or environmental pressure, and it takes the time such translations take.

Who drives without being exempt. It sets direction, and it is subject to the same constraint as everything it directs. This is worth stating because the temptation is to treat will as the one thing that can simply be exercised, standing outside the constraints it operates on. If that were so, every other constraint in this paper would be circumventable by sufficient resolve, and the observable fact is that it is not.

### 3.3 The Lateral Axis: Definition and Space

**What. Potential and individuation.** A thing cannot leap to a higher measure of potential. It translates through intermediate thresholds of capacity, driven by trial, discovery or energetic input.

**Where. Location and matter.** Matter cannot jump between coordinates and must occupy every point between, driven by physical work.

The axis pairs what a thing is with where it is, which is why a state can be rich in one and impoverished in the other. Holding every material needed while having no correct account of what is being held is a real and common condition, and so is the reverse.

### 3.4 The Longitudinal Axis: Function and Meaning

**How. Logic and operations.** A procedure cannot leapfrog its own steps. Progressing through an operation requires computational or physical execution, and a result cannot be had without the execution that produces it.

**Why. Meaning and resonance.** Meaning does not appear spontaneously. A motive translates through intermediate states of relevance, driven by lived friction and accumulated experience.

The Why body is the one most likely to be dismissed as not a real constraint, so it is worth being explicit. An act with no available meaning in its setting is not a live option there, however physically possible it is. This is not a claim that people are prevented from acting against their culture. It is a claim that an act nobody in a setting has a way of understanding as a thing one might do is not in the option set in the sense that matters, because option sets are composed of actions an agent can represent to themselves as actions.

### 3.5 The Vertical Axis: Time and Consequence

**Cause. History and precedence.** A downstream state cannot exist without the unbroken chain of states preceding it.

**Effect. Emotion and consequence.** Emotional and physiological states cannot jump. They translate through intermediate states, driven by time, regulation and processing.

The Effect body is where section 8.3 locates the capacity to forgive, and it is why that capacity behaves like a trained strength rather than a conclusion reached.

### 3.6 What Comes First

The bodies are not independent of one another, and one dependency runs between them.

Five of them hold attributes of an entity, and What names the entity. Until the naming is done, the rest have nothing to be about. Where is what. How does what work. Why did what happen. There is no referent for the remaining questions until the entity is individuated.

The priority is a dependency, not a schedule. Nothing here claims Where must be settled before Why, or How before Cause. Those can be worked in any order and usually are. The claim is narrower and more defensible: nothing binds until What is fixed.

Data can be gathered ahead of the What. Measurements, anomalies and correlations pile up for years. But they attach to nothing until the entity is individuated, and two failure modes follow, corresponding to a What that is false and a What that is absent.

**A false What.** In 1846 Le Verrier noticed Uranus deviating from its calculated Newtonian orbit. He defined the What as an unseen outer planet, calculated its Where from that definition, and sent the coordinates to Johann Galle, who found Neptune within one degree of the predicted position on the first night, 23 September 1846.

In 1859 Le Verrier observed an anomaly in Mercury's perihelion. Using the identical method, he assumed the What was again an undiscovered planet and named it Vulcan. Astronomers searched the calculated Where for decades and found nothing.

The instructive feature is that the method was the same and the outcomes were categorically different. The Vulcan calculation was not less careful than the Neptune one. What distinguishes them is the first component, and the failure mode is worth naming precisely: a Where computed from a false What is not inaccurate. It is empty. There is no object to be nearly right about, so the error cannot be reduced by refining the calculation, which is what fifty years of searching established.

**An absent What.** Semmelweis measured an effect and ran a procedure at the Vienna General Hospital in 1847, ordering examiners to scrub in chlorinated lime, and maternal mortality fell sharply and stayed down. The What remained "cadaverous particles."

The effect was real and the procedure repeatable, and the finding still could not be transferred, defended or extended, because the thing being acted on had not been individuated. Without a What, there was no way to say what else the procedure should apply to, no way to predict where it would fail, and no way to answer an objection, since an objection to an unindividuated cause has nothing to engage. That waited on germ theory.

The two cases show the two halves of one dependency. A false What produces results that point nowhere. An absent What produces results that cannot be carried anywhere.

## 4. Tools

Every change on every body requires force. Force always comes from a tool, and the tool is the input most often treated as though it were free.

### 4.1 Force Comes From a Tool

Section 3 establishes that translation across any body requires applied force. It does not follow that force is available, and treating it as available is the error this section corrects.

Force is always supplied by a tool. Muscle moves matter. An operation moves a calculation. Attention and memory move an information state. Regulation moves an emotional state. Each of these is a made thing with a finite capacity, produced by prior states and subject to the rule.

So every change is gated twice: once by the continuity of the path, and once by the strength of the tool that has to drive it. A path can be perfectly continuous and still not be walked.

This is a distinct way of being stuck, and neither missing material nor missing knowledge describes it. The direction is right, the target is individuated, the route is continuous, and there is not enough strength to cover it. Recognising this as a third condition rather than a variant of the other two matters because the remedies differ: absent material must be supplied from outside, absent knowledge must be acquired, and insufficient strength must be built, which takes time and cannot be shortened by supplying anything.

### 4.2 Self-Developing Tools

Some tools are built by being used, and their development has a shape worth stating precisely.

A muscle is made stronger by use, and using it requires the muscle. So it can only develop from wherever it already is, in increments it can currently sustain. Loading beyond that produces injury rather than acceleration. The same structure holds for an operation practised into fluency, for attention, and for emotional regulation.

This is section 3's rule applied to the tool rather than to the thing the tool moves. No jump from weak to strong. Every intermediate state occupied. Force applied at each step, drawn from the tool as it currently stands.

Aristotle's account of moral virtue has the same structure and predates this by a long way. Virtue is a hexis, which is not a habit in the passive sense but an active condition, a state in which something actively holds itself, and it is acquired by doing the corresponding acts. One becomes just by doing just acts, which is circular if read as a definition and exactly right if read as a description of a self-developing tool: the capacity is what performs the acts that build the capacity.

The convergence matters for two reasons. It shows the structure is not an artefact of this paper's vocabulary, since Aristotle reaches it without any of it. And it extends the claim beyond physical capacities, since what Aristotle is describing is a moral one.

The general result: you cannot train from a strength you do not have, any more than you can build from material you do not have.

### 4.3 Strength on Each Body

Each of the seven bodies has a tool, and each tool has a strength that was built or left unbuilt.

This makes a state readable in a way that listing its contents does not. A state is not only open or closed on a body. It is strong or weak on it, and the distinction between weakness and absence is not a matter of degree. Weakness can be trained from where it is. Absence has to be supplied from outside, because there is nothing to train.

The paired axes make the reading concrete. A state with everything needed in hand and no correct account of what is being held is strong on the Where body and weak on the What. A state where a pattern is legible with no account of how it works is strong on the Why and weak on the How. Naming which body is weak names what has to be trained, which is more actionable than naming what is missing, because the second says only that one must wait.

A man cannot use his forgiveness arm if it is weak.

### 4.4 A Tool Applied to the Wrong Body

Because tools are body-specific, a strong tool can be brought to a problem it cannot touch, and this is a failure mode distinct from having no tool at all.

Semmelweis is the case. His procedural tool was excellent. He designed a controlled intervention, enforced it against resistance, and measured the result with a rigour unusual for the period. The weakness was on the What body, where the entity had not been individuated. A stronger procedure could not fix that, because procedure is a How-body tool and the constrained body was elsewhere.

This failure looks like effort rather than error, which is why it persists and why it is hard to diagnose from inside. Force is being applied, sometimes a great deal of it, and the absence of progress reads as a reason to apply more. But the force is going along the body where the actor happens to be strong rather than the one where the state is shut, and increasing it changes nothing except the cost.

The diagnostic follows from 4.3: identify which body the state is actually constrained on before deciding what to strengthen. An actor strong on one body will reliably misidentify, because the tool they reach for first is the one they have.

### 4.5 Tools Are Lost

Tools do not only fail to be built. They are lost, and a lost tool is not recovered by wanting it.

Dollo's law describes this biologically. Once the developmental pathway underlying a complex trait is gone, the trait does not return in its original form. The modern refinement is that the mechanism is genetic and developmental rather than statistical: it is not that reversal is improbable but that the structure that would have to be reversed is no longer there.

The lineage did not decide against the trait. The tool was discarded under conditions that made it unnecessary, and rebuilding it would require translating back along a route for which no force now exists.

The same structure holds above biology. Operations fall out of practice and the practitioners die. Institutions lose the ability to do what they once did routinely, while retaining the documents describing how it was done. The record of a capability is not the capability, because a capability is a tool with a strength and a record is information, which is a different input.

## 5. Precedence in Practice

Progress is not a series of clever ideas. It is the assembly of prerequisites. Three cases, chosen because in each the missing input is a different one of the three.

### 5.1 Bronze Before Iron: A Missing Tool

Historians sometimes ask why humanity spent over a thousand years in a Bronze Age, given that iron ore is far more abundant in the Earth's crust than copper and tin. The usual answer, that iron needs a hotter fire, is wrong, and correcting it strengthens rather than weakens the case.

Copper melts at 1085°C and bronze alloys lower still, so bronze is a melt-and-cast technology. The metal becomes liquid and is poured, and moulds, pouring and finishing follow from that.

Iron melts at 1538°C. Ancient furnaces topped out around 1100 to 1200°C and could not reach it, so iron was never melted at all in this period. A bloomery reduces iron ore in the solid state, well below its melting point, producing a spongy bloom mixed with slag, and the slag must liquefy at around 1200°C to keep the reduced iron protected and the process moving.

So the barrier was not a temperature. The temperature gap between casting copper and running a bloomery is around a hundred degrees, which is not the difficulty. The barrier is that the accessible temperature ruled melting out entirely, which forced a different class of operation: solid-state reduction, repeated forging to consolidate the bloom and expel slag, then carburising, quenching and tempering.

What centuries of bronze casting built was furnace practice: refractory linings that survive long runs, tuyeres admitting air where it is wanted, bellows and draught control, and charcoal made reliably at scale and close to pure carbon, which supplies the reducing atmosphere that strips oxygen off the ore.

None of those is a material and none is a temperature. They are tools in the sense of 2.2, and the case is a clean instance of the second input being the binding constraint: the ore was abundant and underfoot, and the operation that could work it did not exist.

### 5.2 Newton Before Einstein: Missing Information

Newton's mechanics was not a flawed attempt at General Relativity. It was the exact output of what could be observed and computed in 1687.

Einstein could not have published in 1687 because the informational inputs had not been assembled, and each of them required time that could not be compressed.

Le Verrier's 1859 calculation of Mercury's perihelion shift, which he put at roughly 38 arcseconds per century and Newcomb later revised to about 43, required a century and a half of telescopic observation recorded after Newton. The discrepancy is a residual in a long series. It cannot be observed in a single night or inferred from first principles. It exists only as the output of accumulated measurement, which is to say it had to be made.

The 1887 Michelson-Morley experiment required an interferometer sensitive enough to detect nothing. The null result is the finding; the constancy of light speed is what was inferred from it. A null result of this kind is only informative when the instrument is known to be sensitive enough that it would have detected a signal had one been there, which is itself a fact about instrument development.

Maxwell's 1865 field equations unified electricity and magnetism.

Einstein's physics was assembled out of the precise contradictions that Newtonian mechanics took two centuries to produce. The contradictions are not an embarrassment to Newton. They are the product, and they are what the successor theory is made from.

### 5.3 Carnivory: A Lost Tool

Carnivores did not choose meat in any deliberate sense.

Cats cannot synthesise taurine in sufficient quantity and must take it from diet. Feeding cysteinesulfinic acid as a putative precursor produces no significant increase in taurine in plasma, whole blood or muscle, which establishes that the deficit is a missing pathway rather than a missing substrate. They cannot make arginine. They cannot convert beta-carotene to vitamin A and so require retinol from meat.

Choice here means accumulated causal commitment. A lineage specialised around available fuel, and once specialised the route back closed. This is section 3's constraint running over generations and tool loss in the sense of 4.5: no lineage jumps between dietary states, and the reverse translation requires force that no longer exists in the genome.

The case is included because it shows the account operating where no agent and no deliberation are present at all. If the constraint held only for deliberating agents it would be a claim about rationality rather than about causality.

## 6. The Origin State

Every causal chain traced backward reaches a state with nothing before it. This is not one application of the rule among others. It is the only state with no predecessor, and everything else in this paper is downstream of it.

It is also where every version of the perfection complaint terminates. "Why was this period not better" resolves into "why was the world not made better," which resolves into a complaint about the resolution of an initial state. The small instances are worth stating first because the same move is far harder to see at the largest scale.

### 6.1 Information Before Light

Before the physical universe exists, what is present is informational potential. This is the uncaused term, and 6.2 derives why one is required.

By the rule, whatever is made first must be assembled from what is already present. Since information is the only available material, the first thing made is light.

Light is information.

A source of light is a source of information other than the origin, and can only appear downstream. A source of a thing cannot precede the thing it is a source of, which is section 3's constraint in its first and simplest instance, because at that state there is nothing else in play to complicate it.

This is not an arbitrary narrative and it requires nothing about the physical nature of light at the origin. It is the only ordering the rule permits given what that state contains, and the ordering that appears puzzling from a physical reading is the required one from an informational reading.

Genesis 1:2 states the condition preceding the first act in these terms: the earth formless and void, darkness over the deep, and the Spirit of God hovering over the waters. Only then, in 1:3, does God speak. By the mapping of 2.4, the information leg is what is present at the formless state, and speech is what is made from it.

The transition from formless potential to structured reality took the span it took because each stage had to generate the material of the next. The number of stages is a fact about how many were required, not about how long the maker took, and asking why it was not done at once asks why an end can exist before its parts.

### 6.2 The Uncaused Term

Every made thing takes from what already is. So the sequence either runs back without end, or it terminates in something that makes without taking.

Only something uncaused, depending on nothing before itself, could make without taking. Everything else has to take, because everything else is made of what came before it.

This follows from the rule rather than being assumed by it, and the result is narrower than it may appear. What it establishes is that a slot exists and must be filled. It does not establish what fills it. The uncaused term may be God, a brute initial condition admitting no further explanation, or an unresolved feature of a beginningless chain. Every result in this paper holds on any of these readings, which is worth stating explicitly, because an argument that smuggled theism in as a premise would be worthless to a reader who does not grant it.

### 6.3 Taking Is the Price of Being Finite

Critiques of existence often demand a world where nothing costs anything to anyone. In a finite reality this is not a high bar to clear. It is incoherent.

Life consumes other organised systems to resist entropy. A plant uses sunlight, minerals and water thereby excluded from competing plants. Simply existing as a physical object occupies space nothing else can occupy.

The instinct that some forms of life escape this is worth following to its end, because it terminates. Herbivory does not escape it: eating a plant ends a living process, and that plant was already drawing on a supply something else could have used. Nor does photosynthesis, which draws down a supply the plant did not make. Occupying ground is itself a form of taking, since ground used for one thing cannot be used for another.

Taking is therefore not a design flaw. It is what being a distinct, finite thing in time consists in, and the price of anything besides the uncaused term existing separately at all. Only something that depends on nothing prior could make without taking, which is the result of 6.2 arriving from the other direction.

### 6.4 Taking Is Not One Quantity

That taking is unavoidable does not make all taking equivalent, and the account would be useless if it did.

Two properties of what is taken decide where a particular taking sits.

**Renewability.** Whether the resource replenishes within the timeframe that matters.

**Rarity.** How scarce it is relative to what the local state needs to remain viable.

Taking from something abundant and fast-regrowing and taking from something scarce and slow-regrowing are different acts, though both are literally taking. The first leaves the next state roughly intact. The second narrows it, which means it reduces what the next state can produce, and that reduction is measurable in principle rather than a matter of sentiment.

Mapped onto the two-axis measure, where υ tracks who an act benefits and ψ tracks what the energy is doing, taking does not sit at the destructive end of ψ merely because it is unavoidable. Where it sits depends on whether the take outruns what can regrow, given how scarce the thing actually is there.

### 6.5 The Universe as Locus

To all effect the universe acts like a wand. A locus. A focus where everyone's infinite potential and God's can be gathered and expressed in any form of order at all.

The wand is itself of the universe, so it is limited like every other tool. It gathers and focuses what is there. It supplies nothing from outside, which is 4.1 applied at the largest scale available.

Man does the same through simulations, making universes inside the universe with no rules except self-imposed ones. That is man copying the act that made him. And he does not build one on a whim. He builds it for reasons, and they are the same reasons the universe was made, because the reason is part of what is being copied.

## 7. Human Action

### 7.1 The Actor Index

A causal state cannot be indexed to a historical era. Speaking of what was "possible in 1838" bundles unrelated people into one imaginary option set.

A government official drafting colonial policy in London and a frontier pastoralist facing immediate survival pressure shared no materials, no immediate information, no incentives and no tools. Claiming a choice was "available in 1838" is therefore not merely imprecise. It is true of one of them and false of the other, or true of neither, and the sentence gives no way to tell which.

A state must always be indexed to the actor at their exact coordinates. This matters beyond bookkeeping, because without the index the same evidence supports two incompatible readings and nothing distinguishes them: either every historical outcome is a failure to take an available option, or every apparent failure is somebody's absent option. Both are unfalsifiable. With the index, the coordinates have to be stated, and the question has a determinate answer.

### 7.2 Intention and Conditioning

Actors decide on three bounded parameters: the information they hold, the conditioning and worldview installed by their history, and their incentive structure balancing self-benefit against group- or other-benefit.

In high-scarcity, high-threat environments, survival conditions actors toward self-preservation or immediate tribal interest. In stable, surplus-rich environments, actors gain the room to consider broader outcomes. This is not a claim that scarcity excuses anything. It is a claim about what is in the option set, and option sets are what determine what can be selected from.

The colonisation of Australia illustrates why era-level claims fail. Expansion was the systemic outcome of exploration, trade patterns and imperial directive, and within that movement factions formed: some acting for narrow self-interest, some aiming to establish order for the broader commonwealth, others holding varying degrees of altruism or hostility toward indigenous populations.

The existence of those factions is sometimes taken to show that better options were available to everyone, since somebody took them. It does not, because the factions did not share a state. They differed in information, conditioning, incentive and position, which is exactly what the actor index tracks. A dissenting position held by a person in one set of coordinates is not evidence that it was in the option set of a person in another.

Every actor acted on what seemed viable, necessary or advantageous given their specific conditioning and the information they held.

## 8. Hindsight, Ability, and Forgiveness

A standing objection to any account of this kind is that recognising causal necessity excuses harm. It does not, and the reason is more precise than a disclaimer.

### 8.1 Necessity Is Not an Excuse

One can argue in hindsight that an actor should have done infinite other things in infinite other ways. They did not, because they were acting on, and acted upon by, the exact internal and external information present at that moment.

An excuse tries to absolve by pointing at mitigating factors. It concedes that the act was wrong and asks for the judgment to be softened. BCS does none of that. It describes the chain, and describing a chain issues no verdict to be softened.

A wildfire that burns a town is causally necessary given heat, dry timber, wind and a spark. Saying so is not an excuse for the fire, does not make the destruction desirable, and does not suggest the town should not be rebuilt with firebreaks. A destructive human act is likewise the exact output of the actor's conditioning, information and pressures, and the same three observations hold of it.

There is also a structural reason the account cannot serve as an excuse, which is independent of anyone's intentions in using it. An excuse is offered from the present, about an act being performed or about to be. BCS applies to completed states, for the reason given in 1.4. It does not reach the present. So by the time the claim holds, the state has closed and there is nothing left to excuse, and an actor who reasons "whatever I do will have been the exact output" has mistaken an open state for a closed one, which is not reading the situation but declining to be one of the things that completes it.

### 8.2 The Hindsight Spectrum

In hindsight an entire spectrum of choices becomes visible that was not accessible in real time. The four tiers are distinguished by tools, which makes the boundaries exact rather than matters of degree.

**The Best Impossible Action.** No tool for it exists anywhere, and none could, because it would require laws, energy, materials or mechanisms not present in the universe.

**The Best Improbable Action.** The tool exists somewhere, but this actor's was too weak to drive the translation. Survival threat, conditioning, panic and social pressure are not separate explanations at this tier. They are descriptions of a tool operating at or beyond capacity.

**The Best Possible Action.** The actor's tool was strong enough, on the body where the state was actually constrained.

**The Best Certain Action.** What the tool actually did. The single resultant determined by the exact materials, tool strengths, conditioning and information present at that second.

Looking backward, it is obvious that alternatives were theoretically available, and we naturally agree the actor should have chosen the one leading to the most good. The tiers show what that agreement commits us to.

The principle at stake is the one usually attributed to Kant, that ought implies can: an agent has a moral obligation to perform an action only if it is possible for them to perform it, which the Latin formula gives as *ad impossibilia nemo tenetur*. The principle is contested in its details and its Kantian attribution is debated, but the core is widely held and is what makes the Impossible tier uncontroversial. Nobody thinks an eleventh-century physician was obliged to prescribe antibiotics.

The work this section does is to show that the Improbable tier is on the same side of the line as the Impossible one, and for the same reason. In both, the ability is absent. The difference is only where the absence sits: in the universe, or in this actor. If ought implies can, and this actor could not, then the obligation does not attach, whatever the obligation's standing for a differently equipped actor in the same room.

This is the line where nearly all retrospective judgment goes wrong, and it is not a moral line. It is a measurement of a specific capacity in a specific person at a specific moment, and it is not readable from the outcome. The outcome tells you the action taken. It does not tell you whether the untaken action was within the actor's strength, and assuming it was is the error.

A reader may press: does this not dissolve responsibility altogether? Three things limit the damage.

First, the argument is about ability, not about determinism. It does not claim the actor could not have done otherwise in the metaphysical sense. It claims that a specific alternative was beyond a specific capacity, which is an empirical claim about that person and can be false.

Second, Frankfurt-style cases establish that responsibility does not require alternative possibilities in the first place, so an account that removes alternatives does not thereby remove responsibility. Whether that line succeeds is disputed, but it exists, and BCS does not need to settle it.

Third, and most directly, capacities are built. The Improbable tier is not a permanent exemption. It describes where a tool stood at a moment, and 4.2 says how tools move. An actor who was short of a capacity at one point and remains short of it later, having had the conditions to train it, is in a different position, and nothing here says otherwise.

### 8.3 Forgiveness

The spectrum establishes why forgiveness has a mechanical basis, and where its boundary lies.

Hindsight reveals the actions that would have produced the most good, so the damage is clearly identifiable. Yet the actor was bound by their state. They could not have executed the best possible action, because the necessary information, tool strength or conditioning did not exist in them at that moment. Demanding the past have unfolded differently demands a world that never existed.

**Why anything can be forgiven.** Forgiveness is the recognition that a completed state cannot be altered. It releases the demand for an unpayable debt. There is no category of past action inherently unforgivable, because holding a grievance against a locked state is a demand made of something that cannot answer it.

This is a stronger claim than most philosophical accounts of forgiveness make, and the difference is worth marking. The standard treatments make forgiveness conditional: on repentance, on the offender's change of character, on the victim's overcoming of resentment, or on some combination. Griswold's account is representative in tying forgiveness to conditions both parties must meet. The claim here is not that those conditions are unimportant to reconciliation. It is that they bear on whether the relationship can be repaired, not on whether the past act is in principle forgivable, and the two questions have been run together. The act's forgivability follows from the act being complete, which is a fact about it rather than about anyone's attitude.

**Whether a thing can be forgiven and whether a person can presently forgive it are two questions.** The first is structural and settled above. The second is a matter of strength.

Forgiveness is worked on the Effect body, where by section 3 states cannot jump and must translate under applied force, and by 4.1 that force comes from a tool with finite capacity. Understanding does not supply it. Understanding is a How-body instrument, and applying it harder to an Effect-body constraint is the misapplication of 4.4.

So a person can accept every step of this argument, agree the past state was locked, and still be unable to perform the forgiving, because that arm has not been built. That is not a failure of reasoning and it is not refusal. It is an undeveloped tool, and like any other it is built by use, from wherever it currently is, under force over time.

This distinction does real work, because the standard advice in this territory is to explain to someone why they should forgive. On the account here that advice is a category error with a predictable failure: it applies a How-body tool to an Effect-body constraint, achieves comprehension, and changes nothing, after which the person concludes they are failing at something they understand, which is an accurate description of their position and a useless one.

**Why refusal to recognise being forgiven cannot be forgiven.** There is exactly one boundary and it is definitional rather than moral.

Forgiveness is not a unilateral decree in empty space. It is the clearing of an offense between conscious actors, and clearing is a two-place relation. If someone refuses to recognise the harm, denies the need for reconciliation, or rejects the forgiveness offered, the act cannot take place, in the same way that a gift cannot be given to someone who will not receive it.

You cannot forgive someone who refuses to accept being forgiven. This is not a penalty imposed on them and it is not a limit on anyone's generosity. It is what the word means. An offer that is rejected cannot be consummated, so the actor remains unforgiven by definition rather than by anyone's decision.

One clarification. This does not require the offender's repentance, which would collapse the position into the conditional accounts rejected above. What it requires is only that the offer not be refused. An offender who does not repent but does not reject the clearing is forgiven. An offender who rejects the clearing is not, because there is no one on the other end of the relation.

## 9. Suffering

### 9.1 The Definition

Suffering is acting with a lack of understanding.

Pain is an incoming sensory signal sent when tissue is damaged. Pain lands from the outside.

Suffering is located in the action. It is the friction, error, unintended harm and downstream wreckage generated when an actor acts inside an incomplete information state. It is never located in whoever it lands on. Pain lands. Suffering is done.

### 9.2 Defending the Definition

This is a revisionary definition and the revision should be stated rather than slipped past. In ordinary usage suffering is undergone: the sufferer is the one it happens to, and locating it in the actor rather than the affected party inverts the standard sense.

Three things support the revision.

First, the ordinary usage conflates two things that come apart. What the affected party undergoes is pain, distress, loss and grief, and these are real, are the proper objects of compassion, and are not being denied or renamed. What is left when those are named separately is the production of harm, and that has no ordinary name. Suffering is the available word for it.

Second, the ordinary usage has no account of where suffering comes from, which is why the question "why is there suffering" has been answerable for millennia only by appeal to malice, punishment or mystery. On the definition here it has a mechanism, given in 9.3, and the mechanism is neither malicious nor mysterious.

Third, the revision is tested by what it predicts. If suffering were located in the affected party, then a harm that lands on nobody would not be suffering, and a harm produced with full understanding would be as much suffering as one produced without. Both consequences are wrong on reflection. An act done in ignorance that happens to harm nobody has still done the thing that produces harm, and an act done with full understanding of its effects is something other than suffering, whatever else is wrong with it.

A reader who rejects the definition can read sections 9 and 10 as being about a distinct phenomenon that needs a name, and nothing in sections 1 through 8 depends on it.

### 9.3 Why It Is Built Into Time

An actor's information is bounded by the history accumulated up to that moment. No actor in the present can hold knowledge that only later states will produce, because that knowledge does not yet exist to be held.

All action occurs in the present, and the present is by definition the position where the state is incomplete. So every action is launched from a partial state, and every action carries blind spots, miscalculations and unintended effects.

The conclusion is stronger than it first appears. Suffering is not an occasional event within the sequence, produced by particular failures. It is a property of sequencing itself, because acting from a partial state is the only kind of acting available inside time. There is no version of a temporal agent that acts from complete information, and the demand for a world without suffering, so understood, is the demand for a world without sequence.

### 9.4 It Is Symmetrical

Two parties can each be aiming at what they take to be good, each acting on the best information available to them, and harm is produced by both, with nothing overriding anywhere in the chain.

That is the ordinary case, not the tragic exception. Where it looks as though one party aims at good and the other at bad, that reading is usually an artefact of a partial information state held by whoever is doing the reading, which is itself an instance of the same mechanism operating one level up.

### 9.5 The Tool Question

The question that follows is whether a tool can be made that cannot be used to create suffering in others. Since the universe is that locus, the question is about the universe.

The answer follows from the definition. The tool never creates suffering, because the tool has no understanding to lack. Only the one holding it does. There is nothing that could be built into the wand to fix this, because the thing that produces suffering was never in the wand.

Understanding is itself a tool, and by 4.2 it is built by use from wherever it already is. So the gap between the understanding a person holds and the understanding an act requires is where suffering is produced, and it cannot be closed by being told to close it, for the reason given in 8.3.

## 10. Mistakes and Regret

### 10.1 A Mistake

A mistake is an objective error located inside a single completed state.

When Le Verrier proposed Vulcan he made an empirical mistake in the What. His model did not match the universe. Identifying that requires no alternate reality and no counterfactual. It is demonstrated by comparing his prediction against the actual sky.

A mistake is closed, inspectable and informative, and nothing in this paper makes any error less of an error.

### 10.2 Regret

Regret, in its strict form, is not an empirical evaluation. It is an impossible comparison.

The strict form claims: given the exact same situation, I should have acted differently.

Returning to the exact same situation means the same tired brain, the same missing knowledge, the same emotional pressures, the same conditioning and the same tool strengths. Given that identical configuration, the choice could not have been different, so the claim compares a completed state against a second state that does not and could not exist.

It is also misdirected work. By section 9, regret is an act launched from a partial state, which makes it a producer of the very thing it is reacting to.

**The weaker form.** Regret is often meant in a looser sense: given what I now know, that was bad. This version is coherent and nothing above touches it, because it makes no claim about what was available at the time. It is a present evaluation of a past outcome using present information.

But the weaker form is not what people do when they regret, and the distinction matters practically rather than only terminologically. The weaker form terminates: the outcome is judged bad, the judgment is made, and there is nothing further to do with it except learn. What people describe as regret does not terminate. It recurs, and its recurrence is driven by the strict form underneath, which keeps proposing an alternative that would have required a different actor.

The test for which form is in play is whether the thought settles. A judgment that settles is the weaker form and is correction beginning. A judgment that returns is the strict form, and it returns because it is asking for something that cannot be supplied.

### 10.3 Correction

The only functional response to an error is correction.

Regret stares backward at a completed state that cannot be reopened. Correction takes that state, mistakes included, and uses it as material for the one being assembled now.

When an actor recognises an error, that recognition is new information. It is an input, and it enters the present state as one. The actor takes it and uses it as stock to assemble a better action now, and the past error stops being an unpayable debt and becomes material.

Regret treats a completed state as though it were still open. Correction treats it as stock.

## 11. Conclusion

The account rests on one claim: anything new is assembled from what is present in the immediate prior state, and the output is the exact resultant of those inputs.

Three things give it force beyond the near-tautology it resembles. The inputs are three rather than one, and they are independent, so a state can fail on any of them while succeeding on the others. The constraint holds on every kind of state rather than only on matter, so no body is exempt from the requirement of continuous translation. And the force that drives every translation comes from a tool that is itself made, finite, and in most cases built by use, which means capacity is bounded in the same way material is.

What follows divides into results about the world and results about judgment.

About the world: the sequence terminates in something uncaused, taking is the cost of finitude rather than a defect in it, and suffering is a property of sequencing rather than an intrusion into it.

About judgment: era-level claims about what was available are not merely imprecise but undecidable, the line between what an actor could not do and what they merely did not do is a measurement of capacity rather than a moral verdict, forgiveness has a basis that does not depend on the offender's repentance and a boundary that is definitional, and regret in its strict form asks for an actor who did not exist.

The account does not show that the world is good. It compensates nobody. It supplies no reason why any particular harm had to occur. What it removes is a single move: the comparison of what was made against an alternative that would have required inputs that were not there. That move has done a great deal of work in a great many arguments, and it does not refer.
